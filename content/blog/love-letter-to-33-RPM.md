Dear 33 ⅓ RPM,

I have been unfaithful. I have flirted with your younger, digital, and cloud-bound siblings. They are quicker, more convenient, and never hold a grudge when I change positions mid-song. But they are plagued by ads, especially your youngest sibling, Spotify. To hell with open relationships. I am ready for a commitment.

I long for the ritual—a meticulous foreplay that must precede the first note. It begins with a deliberate choice: the slow burn of Enya’s Celtic melodies or the hardcore brevity of a jazz quickie. Then comes the tactile choreography: massaging the record jacket while it rests like a hip bone in my hands, inhaling its faint scent of aging paper, and feeling the breathless anxiety of sliding the 180-gram circular slab of wax from its inner sleeve—hoping my fingernails leave no permanent scars on its delicate skin. I balance the vinyl masterfully by its outer edges, brush away any faint veils of dust, and slide it down the spindle. I lower the diamond stylus gently into the outer grooves and turn the knob to 33. First comes a faint crackle, then pure bliss capped by a shared climax. At the end, I physically flip your body over to Side B just to finish what we started.

I long for the sound—the rich and raw, uncompressed moaning. Your digital siblings squashed the music’s spectrum, compressing it into tight, sterile MP3 files. But with you, the experience is like peeling back the layers of an onion: every instrument is afforded its own space to breathe. A saxophone and a xylophone stand fully distinct—like searing chili and coconut in the same broth—in a musical array that unfolds in an uninterrupted sequence as intended by the composer. It's a purist's wet dream.

I long for the thrill of the hunt—swiping through Discogs like an online dating app or sweating in the claustrophobic aisles of cramped record shops. I miss the rhythmic clack-clack of hands fingering row after row of worn album jackets and the low, competitive chatter of fellow scavengers. I long for the mystery of blind dates in dusty aisles, leaving with unexpected gems—secondhand treasures carrying the ghosted memories of their exes, like a former lover’s name penned in faded ink across the jacket corner. These wax relics are time capsules that double as perfect pillow-talk fodder, like my most expensive lover: an original pressing of Passenger’s All the Little Lights.

I long to caress and sprawl out your unadulterated gatefolds, transforming my living room floor into a private art gallery. I crave tracing heavy printed artwork like fresh tattoos on bare skin, feeling the matte texture and accumulated dust makeup beneath my fingertips. I pull out thick lyric inserts, studying and voicing the words like sacred incantations. I even admit to using AI to decode the alien Japanese characters on imported obi strips, desperate to unlock every last secret.

The financial toll of our romance is undeniable—a beautiful financial ruin. The cost of our first date alone, from buying sturdy shelves to serve as your reinforced bed, was extortionate and indulgent. Yet I pay the cost for this privilege without a shred of regret. As the old adage goes, love is blind—and perhaps deaf to flaws. I don’t mind your physical limitations: your soft, inaudible nature and desperate need for preamps; the engineering compromises that tame the wildest, deepest bass lines so the needle won’t jump out of your grooves before climax; or the way you tire near the center spindle, fighting inner-groove distortion as the music tightens and strains in its final moan. Those surface imperfections, pops, and crackles aren't noise—they are merely the snores and racing heartbeat of a needy lover who is beautifully and humanly flawed.

Sometimes, I long for occasional 45 RPM flings—quick, intense romantic sprints far removed from our long, deliberate encounters. And 78 RPM? A total drama queen: a tempestuous lover from my grandfather’s youth, a reckless 10-inch storm that spins far too fast, demands specialized attention, and threatens to break my heart—and itself—at every turn if I so much as dare direct my gaze back to you.

Even when the final notes fade and the stylus spirals into the dead wax of the run-out groove, I linger, tracing the secret messages and master numbers etched into your dark margins. Every encounter is a welcome seduction that demands submission but guarantees pleasure. You spin slow, but you hold me longer.

Yours in the groove,

John
